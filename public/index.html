Each check is split into three or four parts. A means the public evidence shows every part. B means one part is missing, D means only one part is shown, and C is in between. F means none, or evidence of the opposite. Open a square to see which parts.

An A needs a written policy or the contract wording. One public statement, such as an interview comment or a statement reported in the press, earns B at most. An F can rest on credible reporting, such as a news report quoting staff. When it does, the evidence says so and includes the lab’s response.

Goals

The scorecard is a start, not the point. Here's what Lab Canary is pushing for.

  1. Labs publish their terms. Every lab publishes its standard employment, exit and equity terms and its speak-up policy, so no one has to guess.
  2. Labs adopt the standard. Every lab meets all seven clauses. The model wording below is there so saying yes is easy.
  3. Labs say so, and keep saying so. Each lab posts a dated statement that it meets the standard and renews it every quarter. If a statement goes missing, that says something too.
  4. Someone independent checks. A whistleblowing lawyer reads each lab's terms and confirms they match what the lab says.
  5. People ask before they sign. Anyone offered a job at a lab can check its terms here and ask about anything that's missing before they sign.

The standard

Seven principles a lab's staff contracts should meet, and the yardstick for this scorecard. Each comes with draft model wording a lab could adopt, so saying yes is easy. The wording hasn't been reviewed by a lawyer yet and isn't legal advice. Employment law differs between countries, so anyone drafting or signing a contract should get their own lawyer.

Draft

I'm not a lawyer, and no lawyer has checked these documents yet. They're a starting point, not legal advice. Employment law differs between countries, so a lab adopting them, or anyone signing them, should get their own lawyer.

1. Confidentiality agreement

Meets C1, C4 and C7

What staff sign when they join. It protects real secrets and nothing else.

1. What counts as confidential

“Confidential Information” means non-public information such as model weights, source code, unreleased research, security details, and customer or personal data. It does not include your opinions, or information about Example Lab's conduct, safety practices, working conditions or possible wrongdoing, except where sharing it would also reveal Confidential Information as defined above.

2. Keeping it confidential

You agree not to share Confidential Information with anyone outside Example Lab, while you work here or afterwards, unless section 3 allows it or Example Lab agrees in writing.

3. What you can always do

Nothing in this agreement or any Example Lab policy stops you from reporting possible breaches of the law, or risks to public health or safety, to any government agency, regulator, legislator, law enforcement body or court; from speaking to a lawyer; or from receiving a reward for information given to a government agency. You don't need to tell Example Lab or ask its permission first. This applies even if the report has to include Confidential Information.

4. This agreement isn't secret

You may tell anyone that you have signed this agreement and what kind of agreement it is, and you may share its full text with your lawyer, financial adviser, immediate family or any government agency. No term of this agreement is itself confidential. Example Lab publishes the standard version at examplelab.example/terms.

2. Criticism and non-disparagement terms

Meets C2

What the employment contract and any leaving agreement say about criticising the company.

In your employment contract

Nothing in this agreement stops you from making truthful statements about Example Lab, including criticism of its conduct, safety practices, ethics or compliance with the law. Example Lab does not ask anyone to agree to a non-disparagement term when they join or while they work here.

In a separation agreement, only if you choose

When you leave, Example Lab may offer you a separation agreement with a mutual non-disparagement term. Mutual means Example Lab makes the same promise about you. The term is optional, is given in return for benefits you would not otherwise receive, ends 24 months after you leave, and never applies to statements about safety, ethics, risks to the public or compliance with the law. You keep everything you have already earned whether or not you sign.

3. Equity terms

Meets C3

The part of the equity plan that says when shares can be taken back.

1. Your equity can't be used against you

Your vested equity and earned pay will not be forfeited, cancelled, bought back below fair market value, or excluded from any tender offer or sale because you raised a concern, criticised Example Lab or made a protected disclosure. Keeping your vested equity, or taking part in a tender offer or sale, never depends on signing a separation agreement.

2. The only reasons equity can be forfeited

Example Lab may only forfeit vested equity if you (a) sold or deliberately leaked Confidential Information, other than in a report the confidentiality agreement allows, or (b) committed fraud or theft against Example Lab. It must first give you its reasons in writing and a fair chance to respond.

These two reasons are examples. A real lab would list its own, and the list should stay this narrow.

4. Speak-up policy

Meets C5 and C6

The whistleblowing policy, published in full, not just summarised in a code of conduct.

1. Who can use it

Anyone who works or has worked for Example Lab: staff, contractors, interns and former staff.

2. What you can raise

Anything you believe is illegal, unsafe or unethical, or a risk to the public. That includes how models are trained, tested and released, and any gap between what Example Lab says publicly about safety and what it does.

3. How to raise it

You can raise concerns through the Speak-Up Line, which accepts anonymous reports. Reports go to the board's Safety and Audit Committee, which is independent of executive leadership. You can also talk to your manager, but you never have to.

4. You can always go outside

You never have to use this policy first. Nothing in it, or in your contract, stops you from reporting to a regulator, government body, law enforcement or a lawyer at any time.

5. No retaliation

Example Lab will not retaliate against anyone who raises a concern in good faith, and treats retaliation as serious misconduct. Retaliation includes dismissal, demotion, cuts to pay or equity, a bad reference and legal threats.

6. What happens next

The committee confirms it has your report within 7 days, looks into it, and tells you the outcome where it can.

7. Keeping it public

This full policy is published at examplelab.example/speak-up. Any change to it will be published within 30 days.

5. Dated statement

Renewed every three months

The canary: a short public statement that everything above is still true. If it isn't renewed, that says something too.

Statement for 1 January to 31 March 2027

Example Lab confirms that, on the date below:

  1. Its standard employment, separation and equity terms and its speak-up policy are published at examplelab.example/terms.
  2. Those terms meet all seven clauses of the Lab Canary standard.
  3. No current or former member of staff is held to older terms that fall short of the published ones.
  4. It has not enforced, or threatened to enforce, any agreement against anyone for raising a concern, criticising the company or making a protected disclosure.

Signed: General Counsel, Example Lab. Date: 1 January 2027. Next statement due: 1 April 2027. If no new statement is published by then, treat this one as expired.

Worried about your own contract?

If you work at an AI lab, or used to, and you're not sure what your contract lets you say, these groups can help. They're independent of the labs and of Lab Canary. Please don't send your contract to me.

  • The Signals Network. A US non-profit that gives free support, including legal advice, to people thinking about speaking up over something that affects the public. It works in the US and internationally, and has told me it's fine to point lab staff to it. It decides case by case what help it can give.
  • Psst.org. Offers tech and AI workers a legally privileged conversation with a lawyer.
  • Protect. Free, confidential whistleblowing advice in the UK.

Use your own phone or computer and a personal account, not anything from work. Clicks in this section aren't logged, and it's blanked out in the session recordings described under “What do you track?” below.

    Q&A

    “Why did you make this?”

    Hi, I'm Johnny. I made this after reading a BBC report where a Google DeepMind engineer said, "In principle I'm not allowed to criticise Google for the duration of my life", and wondering how the other labs compare. It's a rough first version that I built quickly with help from Claude. To be upfront: I've applied for jobs and fellowships at Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind, and nobody pays for this site. Every lab is rated on public evidence under the same rules, and every rating links to its source, but I'll have got some things wrong. If you spot one, open an issue on GitHub with a public source.

    “How is this different from AI Lab Watch, the FLI AI Safety Index or AIWI?”

    Those mostly look at policies and safety practice. This looks at what’s publicly known about the contracts: non-disparagement, equity, NDAs and confidentiality. AIWI’s Publish Your Policies campaign is the closest, and I cite it a lot.

    “Anthropic and OpenAI are top. Are you tied to them?”

    Fair question. I’ve applied for jobs and fellowships at Anthropic, OpenAI and Google DeepMind, and I say so in why I made this. Nobody pays for it, and I don’t own shares in any AI lab. They’re only on 43% and 46%, and most of their grades are a B, C or D. They’re top because they’ve published more, not because their terms are known to be good.

    “Not public = fail is unfair.”

    I went back and forth on it. Treating it as neutral rewards publishing nothing. It’s a score for openness, not a finding that the terms are bad, and the labs control what’s published, so they can fix it. The site shows “Not public” as a “?”, not an F, so you can see the gap.

    “Why these seven clauses?”

    They’re the bits of a contract that decide whether someone can speak up: what they can say, what they lose if they do, and who they can tell. Happy to hear if one’s unfair or something’s missing.

    “Why rate evaluator contracts too?”

    It’s the same question one step out. Staff contracts decide whether people inside a lab can speak up; evaluator contracts decide whether the outsiders checking a lab can say what they find. Labs are starting to bring in independent evaluators, some with near-employee access, but the terms they work under are mostly private. The six evaluator terms follow GovAI’s proposal that those contracts be published. It’s a beta, scored separately, and never added to the staff score.

    “How can you rate contracts you haven’t seen?”

    Mostly I can’t, and the site says so. 18 of 35 are “Not public”. The rest come from published policies, public statements and reporting, each linked. Staff who joined or left at different times may have signed different versions, so a rating is about the latest public terms, not every contract still in force.

    “Why are Anthropic and OpenAI so close?”

    Equal weights. They get there differently: Anthropic has six grades, all B or D. OpenAI has five, and two are As. Click a lab’s score to see them.

    “US law already bans some of this.”

    Some of it, in some places, for some disclosures. A clause can be unenforceable and still stop people talking, because most people won’t test it. Saying it plainly in the contract is the point of C4.

    “What’s the site made of, and where does it run?”

    One HTML page and three data files, ratings.json and criteria.json for staff contracts and evaluators.json for evaluator contracts, plus update feeds made from the ratings file. There’s no server, database or login. It’s hosted on Cloudflare Pages and the domain is registered with Cloudflare. All the code and every rating are public on GitHub under the MIT licence, with a history of every change. I built it with help from Claude.

    “Who pays for it?”

    Nobody. I pay for the domain, about $10 a year, and the hosting is free. There are no ads.

    “What do you track?”

    I want to see how people use the page so I can make it better. Cloudflare Web Analytics and PostHog (hosted in the EU) count visits, and PostHog also records sessions on this page: clicks, scrolling and mouse movement. Neither sets cookies, PostHog doesn’t keep IP addresses, and nothing you type is recorded. Recordings are deleted after 30 days. I don’t sell or share any of it.

    “Do you keep my email?”

    Only if you sign up for updates. Your address is stored with Buttondown, the newsletter service, and only used to email you when a rating changes. You confirm by email first, every email has an unsubscribe link, and unsubscribing deletes your address. I don’t sell or share it. Use any address you like: you don’t need to use a work one.

    “Can I send you my contract, or a tip?”

    Please don’t. The point is to get labs to publish their terms, not to collect leaks. I only use public evidence, so I’d delete anything confidential without using it, and sending it to a website probably isn’t legally protected. Worried about your own terms? The Signals Network, Psst.org and Protect can help.

    “How do I get in touch, or fix a mistake?”

    Email hello@labcanary.com. Please don’t send contracts or anything confidential. If a rating is wrong or missing, you can also open an issue on GitHub with a link to a public source, so everyone can see the change and why. Labs can do the same, or publish their terms and opt in.
